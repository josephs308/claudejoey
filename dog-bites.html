In Missouri, a dog's owner is generally strictly liable when the dog bites someone in a public place or while the victim is lawfully on private property — which means you usually do not have to prove the owner did anything wrong or knew the dog was dangerous. That's a powerful advantage for bite victims, and Thompson Law STL knows how to use it to pursue the compensation you deserve.

Missouri's strict-liability dog-bite law

Missouri's dog-bite statute, RSMo 273.036, makes a dog's owner generally liable for a bite as long as a few conditions are met. In most cases, the owner is responsible when:

  • The bite happened in a public place, or while you were lawfully on private property (including the owner's property)
  • You did not provoke the dog

Because the law imposes strict liability, you generally don't have to prove the owner was careless. That's very different from states that require proof of negligence. Illinois has a similar strict-liability rule under its Animal Control Act, so bites on the Illinois side of the river are often covered too. As with any legal rule, the specifics vary, so it's worth confirming how the statute applies to your facts with the firm.

No "one free bite": under Missouri's statute, a dog's prior history generally doesn't matter. The owner can be liable even if the dog had never bitten anyone before and seemed perfectly friendly.

What if the owner says I provoked the dog?

Provocation is the main defense to a Missouri dog-bite claim. If the owner can show you provoked the dog, that can reduce your recovery — and under Missouri's comparative fault rules, in some cases defeat the claim. Insurers know this and often raise provocation to pay less, even when it didn't happen. Part of our job is to gather witness statements, medical records, and other evidence that shows what really took place.

Who actually pays a dog bite claim?

Many people hesitate to pursue a claim because the dog's owner is a neighbor, a friend, or a family member. Here's what's important to understand: compensation usually comes from the owner's homeowner's or renter's insurance, not out of their own pocket. Pursuing a claim is generally about accessing the insurance coverage that exists for exactly this situation — not about punishing someone personally.

Dog bite injuries we handle

Dog bites can cause serious, lasting harm that often requires ongoing treatment:

  • Deep puncture wounds and lacerations
  • Infection, which is a common and dangerous complication
  • Nerve and tissue damage
  • Permanent scarring and disfigurement, sometimes requiring reconstructive surgery

Children are among the most frequent and most vulnerable victims. Because they're smaller and closer to a dog's level, bites to the face and head are tragically common, and the scarring can be lifelong. We handle these cases with particular care.

Dog Bites: common questions

General information about Missouri and Illinois law, not legal advice for your situation. Deadlines and rules vary, so call for a free consultation about your case.

Is Missouri a strict liability state for dog bites?

Yes. Under RSMo 273.036, a dog's owner is generally strictly liable for a bite in a public place or while you're lawfully on private property, as long as you didn't provoke the dog — so you usually don't have to prove the owner was careless. Specifics vary, so confirm how the law applies to you with the firm.

What if the dog never bit anyone before?

Under Missouri's strict-liability statute, prior history generally isn't required. Unlike the old "one free bite" idea, you usually don't have to show the dog bit someone before or that the owner knew it was dangerous.

Who actually pays a dog bite claim?

In most cases, compensation comes from the owner's homeowner's or renter's insurance, not out of their pocket. Pursuing a claim is usually about accessing that coverage — not about financially ruining a neighbor or friend.

What if they say I provoked the dog?

Provocation is a defense, and comparative fault can reduce or in some cases defeat a claim. Insurers often raise it to pay less, so we gather witness accounts and other evidence to show what really happened.
