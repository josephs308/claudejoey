If you were hurt in a motorcycle crash that someone else caused, you have the same right to full compensation as anyone else on the road — even though insurers and jurors often assume the rider was to blame. The truth is that most motorcycle accidents are caused by other drivers who fail to see or yield to a rider. At Thompson Law STL, we fight the "reckless biker" narrative and build your case on the facts.

The bias riders face — and how we fight it

From the moment a motorcycle crash is reported, riders are fighting an uphill battle against assumptions. Adjusters, and sometimes jurors, start from the belief that the motorcyclist was speeding, weaving, or taking a risk. We counter that head-on with hard evidence — the crash scene, the vehicle damage, witness accounts, and when needed, accident reconstruction — so the case is decided on what actually happened, not on a stereotype.

Most motorcycle crashes are caused by other drivers

Despite the reputation, the great majority of serious motorcycle collisions trace back to a driver's failure to see or yield to the rider. Common causes include:

  • Left-turn collisions — a driver turns across the rider's path at an intersection
  • Unsafe lane changes — a driver merges into a rider in a blind spot
  • Following too closely — a tailgating driver can't stop in time
  • Failure to yield, running a light or stop sign, and misjudging a rider's speed
  • Distracted, drowsy, or impaired driving

Watch out: after a motorcycle crash, the other driver's insurer may try to get you on record and nudge you toward saying you were "going too fast" or "came out of nowhere." Don't give a recorded statement before you talk to a lawyer — those words are often used to shift blame onto the rider.

Motorcycle injuries are often severe

With far less protection than a car's occupants, riders tend to suffer more serious harm even in lower-speed crashes. These injuries can mean long recoveries and significant costs, which is why full documentation matters so much:

  • Road rash and serious soft-tissue damage
  • Broken bones and crush injuries
  • Spinal cord injuries, including paralysis
  • Traumatic brain injuries (TBI)

The helmet question in Missouri

Missouri law generally requires motorcyclists to wear a helmet, though since 2020 riders 26 and older who carry qualifying health insurance may ride without one. Importantly, not wearing a helmet does not automatically bar you from recovering for your injuries. An insurer may try to raise it to reduce what it pays, and how it affects a specific claim depends on the facts — so it's worth reviewing your situation with us.

Comparative fault — partial blame is not the end of your claim

Missouri follows a comparative-fault rule. In general, that means if you are found partly at fault, your recovery is reduced by your share of the fault rather than eliminated entirely. Insurers often exaggerate a rider's share of the blame to pay less, so having an advocate who can push back on that number can make a real difference. As always, how the rule applies depends on the specific facts, which we'll go through with you.

Motorcycle Accidents: common questions

General information about Missouri and Illinois law, not legal advice for your situation. Deadlines and rules vary, so call for a free consultation about your case.

Who is usually at fault in a motorcycle accident?

More often than not, the other driver. Many motorcycle crashes happen because a car or truck driver fails to see or yield to the rider — turning left across their path, changing lanes into them, or following too closely. Insurers like to blame the rider, but we build the case on the physical evidence and the facts of what actually happened.

Can I still recover if I wasn't wearing a helmet?

Generally, yes. Missouri law generally requires motorcyclists to wear a helmet, though since 2020 riders 26 and older who carry qualifying health insurance may ride without one. Not wearing a helmet does not automatically bar you from recovering, although it can become an issue the insurer raises, and how it affects your claim depends on the facts.

Does being partly at fault stop me from recovering?

Usually not. Missouri follows a comparative-fault rule, which means your recovery is generally reduced by your share of fault rather than eliminated. Even if the insurance company claims you were partly responsible, you may still recover. How the rule applies depends on the facts, so it is worth having us review your case.

How long do I have to file a motorcycle accident claim?

In Missouri, most injury claims must generally be filed within five years of the crash; in Illinois it is generally two years. These deadlines and their exceptions vary with the facts, so confirm your specific deadline with our office as soon as you can.
