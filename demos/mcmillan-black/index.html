Common questions

General information about Texas law, not legal advice for your situation. Call for a free consultation about your case.

What can I do if the other side backs out of a Texas real estate closing?

It depends on your contract. Under the standard Texas (TREC) residential contract, if the seller defaults the buyer can usually either sue to force the sale or terminate and get the earnest money back. If the buyer defaults, the seller can usually enforce the contract, seek other relief or keep the earnest money. Act quickly, because the contract's notice and title deadlines keep running.

My contractor's work is defective. Can I sue right away?

Usually not right away. For most residential construction defect claims, the Texas Residential Construction Liability Act requires you to give the contractor written notice at least 60 days before filing suit, and to let them inspect and offer a repair. Document the defects with photos and keep every invoice and message.

A contractor filed a mechanic's lien on my house. What should I do?

Do not ignore it. Texas mechanic's liens must meet strict notice and filing requirements, and a lien that does not meet them may be challenged and removed. Gather your contract, invoices, payment records and any notices you received, and have a construction lawyer review them before you pay or sign anything.

Do I need a lawyer to probate a will in Texas?

In most cases, yes. Texas courts generally require an executor or administrator to be represented by an attorney in probate proceedings, and contested estates almost always involve litigation between heirs.
