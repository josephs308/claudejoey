Medical malpractice happens when a health-care provider's care falls below the accepted professional "standard of care" and that failure causes injury. A disappointing or even tragic outcome, on its own, is generally not malpractice. To have a case, you usually must show two things: that the provider deviated from what a reasonably careful provider would have done, and that the deviation actually caused your harm. Thompson Law STL exists to prove both — and to stand up to the hospitals and insurers who fight these claims hard.

What counts as medical malpractice?

Medicine is difficult, and not every bad result is someone's fault. The law recognizes that. The question is not whether treatment failed, but whether the provider's care fell below the standard a competent professional would have met in the same situation — and whether that specific failure is what hurt you. Proving that connection, called causation, is often the hardest part, which is why these cases turn on medical records and qualified expert opinion rather than on hindsight alone.

Common types of medical malpractice

Malpractice can arise almost anywhere care is delivered. Some of the situations we see most often include:

  • Misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis — a missed cancer, heart attack, infection, or stroke that costs precious treatment time
  • Surgical errors — operating on the wrong site, leaving an instrument behind, or avoidable intraoperative injury
  • Medication and dosing errors — the wrong drug, the wrong dose, or a dangerous interaction that was missed
  • Birth injuries — harm to a mother or baby from mismanaged labor and delivery
  • Anesthesia errors — improper dosing or inadequate monitoring during a procedure
  • Failure to monitor — not watching, charting, or responding to a patient's deteriorating condition

Why these cases require a medical expert

Medical malpractice claims are complex and expensive to pursue, and Missouri law reflects that. In most cases the plaintiff must file an affidavit from a qualified health-care provider — often called a health-care affidavit — confirming that the claim has merit before the case can move forward. Because of that requirement, independent expert review is not optional; it is the foundation of a strong claim. Expert review is expensive, but you don't pay for it out of pocket: the firm advances these case costs and recovers them only if we win, so the strength of your case never depends on what you can afford up front.

Missouri's deadline is short — and unforgiving

This is the single most important thing to know: Missouri's statute of limitations for medical malpractice is generally two years, which is shorter than the deadline for many other injury claims. Limited exceptions exist, but you should never assume one applies to you. If you wait and the deadline passes, the court can throw the case out no matter how strong it is.

Don't wait to get advice. Deadlines and their exceptions vary with the facts and must be confirmed with the firm. Because malpractice evidence lives in records that take time to gather and have reviewed, the practical window to build your case is even shorter than the deadline suggests.

Who can be held responsible?

Responsibility is not limited to a single doctor. Depending on what happened, we may pursue any provider or institution whose negligence contributed to the harm, including:

  • Physicians, surgeons, and specialists
  • Nurses, physician assistants, and other clinical staff
  • Anesthesiologists and pharmacists
  • Laboratories and imaging providers
  • Clinics, surgical centers, and hospitals

Part of our job is identifying every party that may share responsibility — and the insurance coverage behind each one.

Questions & Answers

Medical Malpractice: common questions

General information about Missouri and Illinois law, not legal advice for your situation. Deadlines and rules vary, so call for a free consultation about your case.

How do I know if I have a medical malpractice case?

A bad medical outcome by itself is not malpractice. You generally have a case only if a provider's care fell below the accepted standard of care and that deviation actually caused your injury. Because proving both points takes medical records and expert review, the best first step is a free case review so we can evaluate what happened.

How long do I have to file a medical malpractice claim in Missouri?

Missouri's statute of limitations for medical malpractice is generally two years, which is shorter than the deadline for many other injury claims. Limited exceptions can apply, so it is important to confirm your specific deadline with an attorney right away — waiting can bar your claim entirely.

Do I need a medical expert to sue for malpractice?

Yes. Missouri law generally requires the plaintiff to file an affidavit from a qualified health-care provider confirming the claim has merit, so independent expert review is essential. We arrange and advance the cost of that review as part of building your case.

What will a medical malpractice lawyer cost me?

Nothing up front. We work on contingency — no fee unless we win — and the firm advances the case costs, including the expert review these claims require. The consultation is always free.
