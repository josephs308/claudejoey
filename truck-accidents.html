If you were seriously injured in a crash with a commercial truck in Missouri or Southern Illinois, you may be able to recover compensation from several parties — not just the driver, but the trucking company, a maintenance contractor, the cargo loader, a freight broker, or a parts manufacturer. Truck wrecks cause some of the most devastating injuries on the road, and the companies behind the truck move quickly to protect themselves. Thompson Law STL moves faster — to protect you.

Truck accident cases are not just bigger car accidents

Commercial trucking is a heavily regulated industry, and that changes everything about how these cases are investigated and proven. Semi-truck claims differ from ordinary car crashes in several important ways:

  • They are governed by federal FMCSA safety regulations covering hours-of-service limits, vehicle maintenance, and driver qualification.
  • They often involve multiple potentially liable parties rather than a single at-fault driver.
  • Large commercial insurance policies mean well-funded, aggressive defense teams from day one.
  • Trucking companies dispatch rapid-response investigators to the scene immediately to begin building their defense.

Who can be held liable

One of the most important early steps is identifying everyone who may share responsibility — because each may carry separate insurance coverage. Depending on what caused the crash, that can include:

  • The truck driver, for unsafe or distracted driving or violating hours-of-service rules
  • The trucking company, for negligent hiring, training, scheduling, or supervision
  • A maintenance contractor, for poorly serviced brakes, tires, or other systems
  • The cargo loader or shipper, for improperly secured or overweight loads
  • A freight broker, for hiring an unsafe carrier
  • A truck or parts manufacturer, for a defective component

The evidence can disappear fast — so act quickly

Modern trucks generate a wealth of electronic evidence, and much of it is often decisive in proving what happened. It can also be overwritten or lost within days if no one acts to preserve it. Critical evidence includes:

  • The driver's electronic logging device (ELD) and hours-of-service logs
  • The truck's engine control module — the ECM "black box" — recording speed, braking, and more
  • Dashcam and nearby surveillance video
  • Maintenance, inspection, and repair records

When we take on a truck case, one of the first things we generally do is send a legal preservation (spoliation) letter requiring the trucking company to retain this evidence. The sooner you call, the more we can protect.

Watch out: the trucking company's rapid-response team may already be at the scene photographing, measuring, and interviewing witnesses before you've even left the hospital. Their job is to limit the company's exposure — not to look out for you. You deserve an advocate working just as fast on your side.

Truck Accidents: common questions

General information about Missouri and Illinois law, not legal advice for your situation. Deadlines and rules vary, so call for a free consultation about your case.

Who can be held liable in a truck accident?

Often more than one party. Depending on what caused the crash, responsibility can fall on the truck driver, the trucking company, a maintenance contractor, the company that loaded the cargo, a freight broker, or the maker of a defective truck part. Identifying every liable party early is critical, because each may carry separate insurance coverage.

What evidence matters most, and why must I act fast?

Electronic data is often decisive: the driver's hours-of-service logs (ELD), the truck's engine control module or black box, dashcam video, and maintenance records. Much of this can be overwritten or lost within days, so we generally send a legal preservation (spoliation) letter right away to require the trucking company to keep it. The sooner you call, the more evidence we can protect.

How long do I have to file a truck accident claim?

In Missouri, most injury claims must generally be filed within five years of the crash; in Illinois it is generally two years. These deadlines and the exceptions to them vary with the facts, so confirm your specific deadline with our office as early as possible.
