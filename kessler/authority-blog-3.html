How Much Is My Personal Injury Case Worth in New York?

By Daniel Kessler, Esq. 18 April 2025 8 min read

The short answer

Case value in New York turns on injury severity, how clear liability is, and the effect on your life. Broad ranges: minor soft tissue $15,000–$75,000; herniated disc requiring surgery $150,000–$500,000+; traumatic brain injury $500,000–$3,000,000+; spinal cord injury $2,000,000–$20,000,000+. Pain and suffering, which New York does not cap in personal injury cases, typically runs 1.5 to 5 times total medical bills.

— Daniel Kessler, Esq. · Kessler & Associates, New York

The question everyone asks, and almost nobody answers

“What is my case worth?” is the first thing an injured person wants to know, and the question most attorneys deflect with some version of “it depends.” That is not good enough. Here is how damages are actually calculated in New York, what moves the figure in each direction, and why the first offer from a carrier is almost never the right number.

Two categories of damages

Every New York personal injury claim contains two kinds of recoverable loss.

Economic damages are the losses with paper behind them: medical bills, projected future care, lost wages, reduced earning capacity, property damage. They are built from records — bills, pay stubs, tax returns and expert projections.

Non-economic damages are the human costs that arrive without a receipt: pain and suffering, emotional distress, loss of enjoyment of life, loss of consortium. They are real, legally recognised, and in serious cases they exceed the economic damages.

New York places no cap on non-economic damages in personal injury cases. That is one reason this state produces some of the largest verdicts in the country, and one reason insurers work so hard to resolve cases before a jury sees them.

How pain and suffering gets calculated

No formula produces a precise figure, but two methods are in common use.

The multiplier method takes total economic damages and multiplies by a factor between roughly 1.5 and 5, depending on severity, permanence, the effect on your life and the quality of the proof. A case with $100,000 in medical bills might support $200,000 to $500,000 in pain and suffering.

The per diem method assigns a daily value to the suffering and multiplies by the days it will last. If a jury accepts $150 a day and you will live with the injury for another twenty years — 7,300 days — that is roughly $1.095 million for that element alone.

Experienced trial attorneys put both calculations in front of a jury and let it choose. Carriers know this, which is precisely why they prefer to settle before the arithmetic is ever presented.

What pushes the number up

Permanence. An injury that resolves in six months is worth materially less than one that permanently limits function. Spinal cord damage, traumatic brain injury, amputation and permanent scarring carry lifetime consequences that juries take seriously.

Clear liability. Where negligence is undeniable — a drunk driver, a documented safety violation, prior complaints about the identical hazard — defendants pay more because they can see what a jury will do with it.

Documented effect on your life. Evidence of what the injury did to your work, your marriage, your ability to care for your children, the things you used to do on weekends. This is the most persuasive material in the file and the most commonly under-collected.

Age. A 35-year-old with a permanent injury has more years of consequence ahead than a 65-year-old, which affects both the earning capacity calculation and the non-economic element.

Egregious conduct. Drunk driving, falsified logbooks, an owner who ignored repeated complaints. Where conduct is reckless rather than merely careless, punitive damages may sit on top of compensatory damages.

What pulls it down

Gaps in treatment. Stop treating for six weeks and the defense argues the injury was not serious, or that something else caused your current condition. Consistent treatment is a legal requirement as much as a medical one.

Pre-existing conditions. Prior injury to the same body part is ammunition. It does not end the claim — aggravation of a pre-existing condition is compensable — but it demands careful medical documentation separating what was there from what the accident caused.

Comparative fault. Under pure comparative negligence your recovery drops by your share. At 20% fault on $500,000 of damages, you recover $400,000. Inflating that percentage is standard practice on the other side.

Policy limits. A case can be worth $2 million and collect $100,000 if the defendant carries minimum coverage and holds no meaningful assets. This is why identifying every available layer — umbrella, employer, underinsured motorist — matters before anyone discusses settlement.

Why the first offer is almost never right

Carriers make early offers because they work. Unrepresented claimants consistently settle for less than represented ones, even net of attorney fees.

An early offer arrives before the full extent of the injury is known, before future care is projected, before lost earning capacity is calculated, and before the file is documented. You cannot know what a case is worth in the first weeks. Signing a release then closes it permanently.

The adjuster who calls is trained to be sympathetic, to make the figure sound reasonable, and to create urgency. The expiry date on the offer is a technique, not a deadline.

New York ranges, by injury type

General ranges by case type. Actual value turns on the specific facts, the venue and the insurance available to pay:

  • Minor soft tissue injury, full recovery — $15,000 to $75,000
  • Herniated disc requiring surgery — $150,000 to $500,000+
  • Fractures with complications — $100,000 to $400,000
  • Traumatic brain injury, moderate — $500,000 to $3,000,000+
  • Spinal cord injury or paralysis — $2,000,000 to $20,000,000+
  • Wrongful death — $1,000,000 to $20,000,000+

Illustrative ranges only. Prior results do not guarantee a similar outcome.

These are not promises. They do illustrate why a $45,000 offer two weeks after a serious accident is almost never the correct number.

How to protect what your case is worth

  • Treat consistently, with no gaps in the record
  • Keep a written account of what the injury stops you doing
  • Stay off social media until the case resolves
  • Give no recorded statement to the other side’s insurer
  • Have anything you are asked to sign read by an attorney first
  • Retain a firm that prepares every case for trial — settlements rise when the other side believes you will actually try it

Frequently asked questions

Is there a cap on pain and suffering damages in New York?

No. New York does not cap non-economic damages in personal injury cases. Pain and suffering, emotional distress and loss of enjoyment of life are unlimited, which is why New York produces some of the largest verdicts in the country — and why insurers work so hard to settle before trial.

How is pain and suffering actually calculated?

Two methods. The multiplier method multiplies total economic damages by roughly 1.5 to 5 based on severity and permanence. The per diem method assigns a daily value and multiplies by the days of suffering. We present both and rely on whichever the evidence supports more strongly.

How long does a case take to settle in New York?

Clear liability with documented injuries commonly resolves in 6 to 12 months. Complex or disputed matters take one to three years. Settling early, before the medical picture is established, can undervalue a case by six figures and cannot be reversed.

What is a personal injury case actually worth in New York City?

New York City juries award more than most United States jurisdictions. General ranges: minor soft tissue $15K–$75K; herniated disc surgery $150K–$500K+; traumatic brain injury $500K–$3M+; spinal cord injury $2M–$20M+.
