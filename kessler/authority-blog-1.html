What to Do After a Car Accident in New York City

By Daniel Kessler, Esq. 15 March 2025 6 min read

The short answer

Call 911 and photograph the scene before anything moves. See a doctor the same day even if you feel fine. Report to your own insurer but decline a recorded statement to the other driver’s. Stay off social media. And speak to an attorney before accepting any offer. New York gives you three years to sue — but the evidence that decides the case disappears in days.

— Daniel Kessler, Esq. · Kessler & Associates, New York

The first ten minutes matter more than you think

Most people injured in a New York City collision make at least one mistake in the minutes afterwards — not through carelessness, but because they are frightened, in pain, and running on adrenaline. Insurance adjusters and defense attorneys know exactly what those mistakes look like, and they are trained to look for them in every file.

What follows is the sequence, from impact through the first seventy-two hours. Follow it and you are in the strongest position the facts allow. Depart from it and you may hand the other side the argument that reduces what your case is worth.

Step 1 — Do not move, unless staying is the danger

If your vehicle can be moved safely out of traffic, move it. Do not move yourself or an injured passenger unless there is an immediate threat such as fire, oncoming traffic or water. Moving someone with a spinal injury can convert a recoverable injury into a permanent one. Call 911 first.

When police arrive, give factual answers about what you observed. Do not speculate about fault, do not apologise, and do not say you “didn’t see” the other driver. An apology at the roadside is read as an admission eighteen months later.

Step 2 — Document the scene before it is cleaned up

If you are physically able, photograph or film the following before anything is moved:

  • Every vehicle from several angles, including plates
  • The resting position of the vehicles, before they are pushed aside
  • Skid marks, debris, the signal phase, and the road surface
  • Any visible injury to you or your passengers
  • The other driver’s licence, registration and insurance card
  • Names and phone numbers of every witness who stopped

This evidence has a life measured in hours. Skid marks fade, witnesses leave, the intersection is swept. Your photographs are frequently the only surviving record of how the collision actually happened.

Step 3 — Get medical attention the same day

This is where the most expensive mistake gets made. People feel acceptable at the scene, decline the ambulance, and go home. Two days later they cannot turn their head.

Adrenaline masks pain. Whiplash, soft tissue injury and mild traumatic brain injury often do not present fully for twenty-four to seventy-two hours. If you wait to seek treatment, the adjuster will argue the delay proves the injury was minor — or that something else caused it.

Go to an emergency department, an urgent care clinic or your own physician the same day, or the following morning at the latest. Describe every symptom, including the ones that feel too small to mention. Get all of it into the record in writing.

Step 4 — Report to your own insurer, and say very little

New York is a no-fault state. Your own policy pays your medical bills and 80% of lost wages up to $50,000 through Personal Injury Protection, whoever caused the crash, and you are required to report the accident to your own insurer. You must apply for those benefits within 30 days — miss it and the bills are yours.

You are not required to give a recorded statement to the other driver’s insurer. Do not give one before speaking with an attorney. Those interviews are scripted to produce admissions about fault and about how well you feel.

To your own insurer, give the basic facts: date, location, the other vehicle’s details. Do not speculate about fault, do not characterise your injuries, and do not say you are “fine.”

Step 5 — Go dark on social media

Defense teams monitor claimants’ accounts as a matter of routine. One photograph of you standing at a family dinner three weeks after the crash, holding a drink and smiling, gets shown to a jury as evidence that your injury is not what you say it is. Context does not travel with the image.

Set everything private until the case resolves, and ask friends and family not to post about you or tag you either.

Step 6 — Speak to an attorney before you accept anything

Carriers sometimes make an offer within days of a crash. It is almost always far below the value of the claim, and a signed release closes the case permanently — including for the surgery you learn you need four months later.

Here is what matters most. If your injury meets the serious injury definition in New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) — a fracture, significant disfigurement, permanent consequential limitation, or inability to perform substantially all of your usual activities for 90 of the first 180 days — you step outside no-fault entirely and sue for full damages, pain and suffering included.

In serious cases, pain and suffering alone is routinely worth two to five times the medical bills. The adjuster on the phone knows that figure. They are working on the assumption that you do not.

New York’s deadlines

Three years from the date of the collision to file a personal injury action. If a City vehicle, an MTA bus or any municipal defendant is involved, a Notice of Claim must be served within 90 days and the action filed within one year and 90 days.

CPLR § 214(5) · Gen. Mun. Law § 50-e · 11 NYCRR 65-1.1

These deadlines are close to absolute. Missing one ends a valid case, and no amount of merit revives it.

The short version

What you do in the first seventy-two hours can add or subtract six figures from the value of your claim. Photograph everything. See a doctor today. Keep your accounts private. Give no recorded statements. Sign nothing without having it read first.

Frequently asked questions

How long do I have to file a car accident lawsuit in New York?

Three years from the date of the accident for a personal injury action. A claim against New York City or another government entity requires a Notice of Claim within 90 days and suit within one year and 90 days. Missing these bars recovery permanently.

Does New York no-fault mean I can’t sue the other driver?

No. No-fault covers medical bills and 80% of lost wages through your own PIP coverage to $50,000. If you have a serious injury — a fracture, permanent limitation, or inability to perform your normal activities for 90 of the first 180 days — you break through the threshold and sue for full damages including pain and suffering.

What if I was partly at fault?

New York applies pure comparative negligence. You can recover even at 99% fault, with your award reduced by your share. Adjusters inflate that percentage routinely; evidence is what pushes it back down.

Should I accept the insurer’s first offer?

Almost certainly not. Early offers are made before the extent of the injury is known and before future costs are calculated, and a release closes the claim forever. We review any offer at no cost before a client signs anything.
