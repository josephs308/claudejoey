New York No-Fault Insurance Explained: When Can You Actually Sue?

✎ Sarah Chen, Esq. 📅 April 2, 2025 🕐 7 min read
Quick Answer In New York, no-fault insurance (PIP) covers your medical bills and lost wages up to $50,000 regardless of fault. However, if your injury meets the 'serious injury' threshold under New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) — including fractures, permanent limitations, or disability for 90+ of the first 180 days — you can sue the at-fault driver for full damages including pain and suffering, which is often worth 2–5 times your medical bills. — Sarah Chen, Esq., Kessler & Associates, New York

What "No-Fault" Actually Means in New York

When people hear that New York is a "no-fault" state, they often assume it means they can't sue anyone for a car accident. That's wrong — and it's one of the most costly misconceptions in New York personal injury law.

What no-fault actually means is this: your own insurance company pays your medical bills and a portion of your lost wages after an accident, regardless of who was at fault. This coverage is called Personal Injury Protection (PIP), and New York requires a minimum of $50,000 per person.

The purpose of the system was to reduce litigation for minor injuries. But if your injuries are serious — and "serious" is a defined legal term — you have the absolute right to step outside the no-fault system and file a full personal injury lawsuit against the at-fault driver.

The "Serious Injury" Threshold: The Key to Your Case

New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) defines "serious injury" as one or more of the following:

  • Death
  • Dismemberment
  • Significant disfigurement
  • Fracture
  • Loss of a fetus
  • Permanent loss of use of a body organ, member, function, or system
  • Permanent consequential limitation of use of a body organ or member
  • Significant limitation of use of a body function or system
  • A medically determined injury or impairment of a non-permanent nature which prevents the injured person from performing substantially all of the material acts which constitute such person's usual and customary daily activities for not less than 90 days during the 180 days immediately following the occurrence of the injury or impairment

That last category — often called the "90/180 rule" — is the most frequently used threshold and the most frequently disputed. If your doctor documents that your injuries prevented you from performing your normal activities for at least 90 of the first 180 days after the accident, you can break through no-fault.

Why This Matters: What You Can Recover Once You Break Through

Inside the no-fault system, you're limited to medical bills and 80% of lost earnings up to $50,000 total. No pain and suffering. No compensation for how the injury affected your quality of life. No recovery for what it cost your family.

Outside the no-fault system — once you've established a serious injury — you can recover everything:

  • All past and future medical expenses
  • All past and future lost wages and lost earning capacity
  • Pain and suffering
  • Emotional distress
  • Loss of enjoyment of life
  • Loss of consortium (impact on your relationship with your spouse)

In serious injury cases, pain and suffering alone is routinely worth two to five times the total medical bills. A case that might settle for $50,000 inside no-fault can be worth $500,000 or more once the threshold is crossed.

How Insurance Companies Attack the Threshold

Because crossing the serious injury threshold opens up the defendant's full liability, insurance defense attorneys work aggressively to knock cases back inside no-fault. The most common strategies:

Gap in treatment arguments. If you stopped treating for more than a few weeks and then resumed, defense attorneys argue you weren't actually injured — or that your current symptoms are from a different cause. This is one of the most powerful weapons they have, and it's completely preventable: keep treating consistently until your doctor releases you.

"Pre-existing condition" arguments. Prior injuries, prior MRIs, prior complaints to doctors are all fair game. Defense teams obtain your complete medical history and look for anything they can use to say your current condition predated the accident. Our attorneys know how to distinguish pre-existing conditions from accident-caused aggravation — they're not the same thing legally.

Independent Medical Examinations (IMEs). Insurance companies can require you to submit to an examination by a doctor of their choosing. These doctors are paid by the insurance company to evaluate claimants, and studies consistently show they find injuries to be less serious than treating physicians do. Having experienced attorneys prepares you for what these exams are actually designed to accomplish.

Pure Comparative Negligence: Even Partial Fault Doesn't End Your Case

New York uses pure comparative negligence — meaning even if you were partially at fault for the accident, you can still recover. If you were 30% at fault, you recover 70% of your damages. This is one of the most plaintiff-friendly negligence rules in the country.

Insurance adjusters routinely inflate the claimant's percentage of fault as a negotiation tactic. They'll cite minor things — following too closely, not using a signal, driving in an unfamiliar area — to assign you more blame than the evidence supports. An experienced trial attorney counters this with evidence and expert testimony.

Rideshare Accidents: Uber, Lyft, and the No-Fault Maze

Rideshare accidents in New York involve layers of insurance that confuse most attorneys, let alone injured passengers. Depending on whether the driver was actively transporting a passenger, waiting for a request, or offline, different insurance policies apply — the driver's personal policy, the rideshare company's commercial policy, or a combination.

Uber and Lyft carry $1 million in liability coverage when a driver is actively transporting a passenger. Getting to that coverage requires knowing exactly which policy is primary and how to structure the claim correctly.

The Statute of Limitations in New York

You have three years from the date of the accident to file a personal injury lawsuit in New York. Claims against New York City require a Notice of Claim within 90 days and a lawsuit filed within one year and 90 days.

Do not wait. Evidence is time-sensitive, medical records must be preserved, and witnesses need to be interviewed while memories are fresh. The moment you think you have a serious injury, call an attorney.

What to Do If You Think Your Injuries Are Serious

If your injuries have kept you from working, required surgery, involved broken bones, or significantly affected your daily life, you likely have a serious injury under New York law. Here's what to do:

  • Continue treating with your doctors consistently — do not let gaps appear in your treatment records
  • Tell every doctor about every symptom, including psychological ones
  • Document everything your injury has prevented you from doing
  • Do not give a recorded statement to the at-fault driver's insurance company
  • Call an attorney before the 90-day Notice of Claim deadline if a government entity is involved

Frequently Asked Questions

What exactly is the serious injury threshold in New York?

New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) defines serious injury as: death, dismemberment, significant disfigurement, fracture, permanent loss of use, permanent consequential limitation, significant limitation of a body function, or inability to perform normal daily activities for 90 of the first 180 days after the accident. Meeting any one of these categories allows you to sue for full damages.

How much PIP coverage does New York require?

New York requires a minimum of $50,000 in Personal Injury Protection (PIP) per person — the highest mandatory minimum in the country. This covers your medical bills and 80% of lost wages up to that limit, regardless of who caused the accident.

Can I sue if I was a passenger in a New York car accident?

Yes. Passengers almost never bear fault for a car accident in New York, which puts them in a strong position to claim both no-fault PIP benefits and — if their injuries meet the threshold — pursue a full personal injury lawsuit against the at-fault driver. Kessler & Associates handles passenger injury cases throughout New York City.

What if the at-fault driver is uninsured in New York?

You can claim through your own Uninsured Motorist (UM) coverage. New York requires minimum UM coverage of $25,000/$50,000. Our attorneys at Kessler & Associates know how to stack available coverage sources to maximize total recovery when the at-fault driver lacks adequate insurance.
