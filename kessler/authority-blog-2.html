New York No-Fault Insurance Explained: When Can You Actually Sue?

By Sarah Chen, Esq. 2 April 2025 7 min read

The short answer

No-fault (PIP) covers your medical bills and 80% of lost wages to $50,000 whoever caused the crash. But if your injury meets the serious injury threshold in New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) — fracture, permanent limitation, or disability for 90 of the first 180 days — you can sue the at-fault driver for full damages including pain and suffering, which in serious cases runs two to five times the medical bills.

— Sarah Chen, Esq. · Kessler & Associates, New York

What “no-fault” actually means here

When people hear that New York is a no-fault state, most conclude they cannot sue anyone over a car accident. That is wrong, and it is the most expensive misconception in New York personal injury law.

No-fault means that your own insurer pays your medical bills and a portion of your lost wages after a collision, regardless of who caused it. The coverage is called Personal Injury Protection, and New York requires a minimum of $50,000 per person — the highest mandatory minimum in the country.

The purpose of the system was to keep minor claims out of court. But if your injuries are serious — and “serious” is a defined statutory term, not an adjective — you have an absolute right to step outside no-fault and sue the driver who caused it.

The serious injury threshold

New York Insurance Law § 5102(d) defines serious injury as one or more of the following:

  • Death
  • Dismemberment
  • Significant disfigurement
  • Fracture
  • Loss of a fetus
  • Permanent loss of use of a body organ, member, function or system
  • Permanent consequential limitation of use of a body organ or member
  • Significant limitation of use of a body function or system
  • A medically determined injury of a non-permanent nature that prevents the injured person from performing substantially all of their usual and customary daily activities for not less than 90 days during the 180 days immediately following the occurrence

That last category — the 90/180 rule — is the most frequently used and the most frequently litigated. If your physician documents that the injury kept you from your normal activities for at least 90 of the first 180 days, you are through the threshold.

What changes once you are through

Inside no-fault you are limited to medical bills and 80% of lost earnings, capped at $50,000 combined. No pain and suffering. Nothing for what the injury did to your life. Nothing for what it cost the people around you.

Outside it, once serious injury is established, the full range opens:

  • All past and future medical expenses
  • All past and future lost wages, and lost earning capacity
  • Pain and suffering
  • Emotional distress
  • Loss of enjoyment of life
  • Loss of consortium — the effect on your marriage

In serious cases, pain and suffering alone routinely runs two to five times total medical bills. A claim worth $50,000 inside no-fault can be worth $500,000 or more once the threshold is crossed. Everything the defense does is aimed at keeping you on the wrong side of that line.

How carriers attack the threshold

Gaps in treatment. Stop treating for a few weeks and resume, and the defense argues either that you were not really injured or that something else caused your present condition. It is the most effective weapon they have and it is entirely preventable: keep treating until your physician discharges you.

Pre-existing conditions. Prior injuries, prior imaging and prior complaints are all discoverable. Defense teams obtain your complete history looking for anything suggesting the condition predated the crash. Aggravation of a pre-existing condition is compensable in New York — but only if the records distinguish the two clearly.

Independent medical examinations. The carrier may require you to be examined by a physician of its choosing. These doctors are retained and paid by insurers, and they find injuries less serious than treating physicians do with striking consistency. Knowing what the examination is designed to produce is most of the preparation for it.

Partial fault does not end the claim

New York uses pure comparative negligence. Even if you were partly responsible, you recover; at 30% fault you recover 70% of your damages. It is among the most claimant-friendly negligence rules in the country.

Adjusters inflate your share as a negotiating position, citing following too closely, a missed signal, unfamiliarity with the road. Evidence and expert testimony are what move that number back.

Rideshare: the same threshold, more policies

Rideshare collisions add layers of coverage that confuse most attorneys, let alone passengers. Which policy responds depends on the driver’s app status at impact — carrying a passenger, waiting for a request, or offline — and the answer decides whether you are looking at a personal auto policy or a commercial one.

Uber and Lyft carry $1 million in liability coverage while a driver is actively transporting a passenger. Reaching it requires establishing status and structuring the claim against the correct primary carrier from the outset.

Deadlines

Three years from the date of the accident to file. A claim against New York City requires a Notice of Claim within 90 days and suit within one year and 90 days. And your PIP application is due to your own insurer within 30 days of the crash.

If you think your injury is serious

If you have been kept from work, needed surgery, broken a bone, or lost function you had before, you likely satisfy the statute. What to do:

  • Keep treating consistently — no gaps in the record
  • Report every symptom to every physician, psychological ones included
  • Keep a written record of what the injury stops you doing, week by week
  • Give no recorded statement to the at-fault driver’s insurer
  • Call an attorney before the 90-day Notice of Claim window if any public entity is involved

Frequently asked questions

What exactly is the serious injury threshold in New York?

Insurance Law § 5102(d) defines it as death, dismemberment, significant disfigurement, fracture, loss of a fetus, permanent loss of use, permanent consequential limitation, significant limitation of a body function, or inability to perform your normal daily activities for 90 of the first 180 days. Meeting any single category opens full damages.

How much PIP coverage does New York require?

A minimum of $50,000 in Personal Injury Protection per person — the highest mandatory minimum in the country. It covers medical bills and 80% of lost wages up to that limit regardless of fault, and the application is due within 30 days.

Can I sue if I was a passenger?

Yes, and usually from a stronger position. Passengers almost never bear fault, which leaves them free to claim no-fault benefits and, if the threshold is met, pursue a full action against the at-fault driver.

What if the at-fault driver is uninsured?

You claim through your own Uninsured Motorist coverage, which New York requires at a minimum of $25,000 per person and $50,000 per accident. We also look for resident-relative policies, employer coverage and umbrella layers to stack against the loss.
